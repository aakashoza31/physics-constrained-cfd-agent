\begin{abstract}
Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) requires substantial manual coordination across geometry preparation, meshing, solver setup, diagnosis, and interpretation. Repeating this work across design alternatives limits high-throughput screening and rapid engineering iteration. We present CFD Forge, a programmable agentic CFD framework built around OpenFOAM. The framework connects engineering requests and geometry parameters to case construction, meshing, boundary and initial conditions, solver execution, diagnostics, visualization, and reporting. A large language model reasons across simulation evidence, interprets flow behavior, identifies likely causes of problems, and proposes bounded corrective actions. Modular scientific contracts complement this reasoning with deterministic measurements and acceptance criteria, retaining final \texttt{ACCEPT}, \texttt{REJECT}, or \texttt{INCONCLUSIVE} authority. Demonstrations include a converging-diverging nozzle and a two-dimensional Mach-2 forward-facing step that satisfy their registered criteria. A three-dimensional turbulent cube serves as a diagnostic stress test: quantitative wake analysis exposes continuing lateral-mode development despite comparatively stable mean quantities, preventing premature acceptance. Case files, diagnostic evidence, action histories, and field-evolution visualizations make the workflow auditable. CFD Forge brings execution, reasoning, and scientific assessment into a common workflow, with future applications in high-throughput simulation, active learning, and engineering design optimization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) is central to engineering analysis, but obtaining a useful result requires a sequence of decisions and operations. An analyst prepares a CAD model or parameterized geometry, defines the domain, generates a mesh, selects physical models, and assigns boundary and initial conditions. Solver execution is followed by diagnosis, assessment, interpretation, and reporting. Although individual steps can be scripted, coordinating them remains expertise-intensive. The NASA CFD Vision 2030 study identifies workflow automation as an important requirement for routine computational analysis and design \cite{slotnick2014vision}.

This effort becomes a bottleneck in high-throughput screening and iterative design, where many geometries or operating conditions must be evaluated consistently \cite{forrester2009surrogate,brunton2020mlfluids}. A programmable CFD workflow could turn an engineering objective into a sequence of reusable operations, then return both simulation results and an explanation of their significance. Quantities such as drag, lift, or pressure loss could subsequently guide new evaluations in an active-learning or optimization loop. Recent LLM-assisted CFD optimization work illustrates this broader direction \cite{chen2026optmeta}.

Large language models (LLMs) offer a way to coordinate such operations through natural-language interpretation, reasoning, and tool use \cite{yao2023react,schick2023toolformer}, supported by retrieval of technical information \cite{lewis2020rag}. OpenFOAM provides a programmable solver foundation \cite{weller1998tensorial}. MetaOpenFOAM and MetaOpenFOAM 2.0 automate simulation and post-processing \cite{chen2024metaopenfoam,chen2025metaopenfoam2}; OpenFOAMGPT and OpenFOAMGPT 2.0 develop retrieval-assisted and end-to-end workflows \cite{pandey2025openfoamgpt,feng2026openfoamgpt2}. CFD-specific fine-tuning \cite{dong2025autocfd}, Foam-Agent \cite{yue2026foamagent}, and ChatCFD \cite{fan2026chatcfd} provide further approaches to automation. AI CFD Scientist and PhyNiKCE also address physical reasoning and verification \cite{somasekharan2026aicfd,fan2026phynikce}. These efforts motivate closer integration of workflow execution, diagnosis, and scientific assessment.

That integration requires attention to the meaning of a successful simulation. Solver completion, small residuals, or plausible contours can coexist with incorrect boundary conditions, unresolved numerical errors, or a flow that is still developing. Verification and validation therefore require multiple sources of evidence \cite{oberkampf2002vv,celik2008discretization}. An agent must interpret these signals together and distinguish a useful next action from evidence sufficient to accept the result.

CFD Forge connects engineering intent and geometry parameters to case construction, meshing, boundary and initial conditions, OpenFOAM execution, diagnostics, corrective actions, and reporting. The LLM reasons across solver logs, field diagnostics, conservation measures, and flow histories to interpret behavior, identify likely causes of problems, and propose what should be tried next. Deterministic scientific checks measure explicit criteria, enforce thresholds, and retain final \texttt{ACCEPT}, \texttt{REJECT}, or \texttt{INCONCLUSIVE} authority. This division gives reasoning a substantive role while keeping acceptance reproducible. Modular scientific contracts organize physical assumptions, numerical requirements, and allowed actions so that additional simulation families can be incorporated. The current evaluation uses registered families and parameterized geometries.

We demonstrate the workflow with a compressible nozzle assessed against quasi-one-dimensional theory and a Mach-2 forward-facing step assessed for transient numerical health and conservation. A three-dimensional turbulent cube provides a diagnostic stress test. Although execution completes and mean drag becomes comparatively stable, quantitative force and wake diagnostics reveal continuing lateral-mode growth. The framework uses this evidence to prevent premature acceptance under the stationarity criterion, illustrating why several physical signals must be examined together. The contribution is a programmable CFD workflow that combines evidence-based reasoning with explicit scientific assessment and preserves its decisions through case files, diagnostic records, action histories, visualizations, and reports. This architecture provides a basis for larger simulation campaigns and future integration with active-learning and design-optimization loops.

\section{CFD Forge Framework and Scientific Assessment}
\label{sec:framework}

CFD Forge organizes CFD analysis as a programmable workflow that connects an engineering request to simulation execution, assessment, and reporting. The framework combines language-model reasoning with programmatic case construction and explicit scientific checks. Its central design principle is to separate proposing an interpretation or action from determining whether the resulting simulation satisfies the prescribed acceptance criteria.

\subsection{Workflow Overview}
\label{sec:workflow}

The workflow begins with a natural-language engineering request describing the intended flow problem and relevant geometry or operating parameters. The request is interpreted and matched to a registered simulation family. Each family supplies a scientific contract defining the physical assumptions, admissible inputs, numerical recipe, required diagnostics, and permitted corrective actions.

For an admissible request, a family-specific runner constructs the case, generates the mesh, assigns boundary and initial conditions, and executes OpenFOAM. Diagnostic routines then extract evidence from the mesh, solver logs, saved fields, and time histories. This evidence supports both model-assisted interpretation and deterministic scientific assessment. Where an allowed corrective action is justified, the runner can update the permitted settings or continue execution before reassessing the result. Otherwise, the workflow terminates with a recorded decision and supporting artifacts.

The present implementation uses parameterized geometries and registered CFD families. This provides a concrete interface between engineering intent and executable simulation operations, while allowing additional families to be incorporated through their own scientific contracts and runners.

\subsection{Reasoning and Scientific Authority}
\label{sec:authority}

The LLM provides a flexible interface for interpreting engineering requests and reasoning over simulation evidence. Its responsibilities include explaining observed behavior, identifying likely causes of problems, proposing a next action, and supporting the engineering narrative. The evidence supplied to this reasoning process can include solver-health indicators, field extrema, conservation measures, convergence histories, and family-specific diagnostics.

Scientific acceptance is evaluated separately. Deterministic routines calculate the relevant quantities and compare them with the registered criteria. The LLM can propose that a run appears complete or requires further work, but its statement does not override a failed or unresolved check. Similarly, a proposed correction must be permitted by the family contract before it can be applied.

This separation yields three terminal outcomes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{ACCEPT}: the required checks are satisfied.
    \item \texttt{REJECT}: the evidence violates a required criterion.
    \item \texttt{INCONCLUSIVE}: the available evidence is insufficient
    to resolve the required assessment.
\end{itemize}

Acceptance therefore refers to the stated scientific contract and
the evidence available for that run. It does not imply that every
source of modeling or numerical uncertainty has been eliminated.

\subsection{Scientific Contracts and Corrective Actions}
\label{sec:contracts}

A scientific contract makes the basis for assessment explicit before
a result is accepted. It specifies the geometry and operating range,
physical model, boundary-condition formulation, mesh requirements,
numerical controls, diagnostic quantities, and reference comparisons
applicable to a family. The criteria reflect the intended flow
regime: a steady nozzle requires appropriate stationarity evidence,
whereas an unsteady compressible step requires conservation checks
that account for changes in stored mass.

Corrective actions are restricted to those supported by the registered
workflow. The model proposes an action, while deterministic checks
establish whether it is admissible and whether another solver attempt
is warranted. The resulting case is then assessed using the same
acceptance criteria rather than a revised threshold chosen to match
the outcome.

The archived nozzle demonstration illustrates this sequence: unmet
stationarity and mass-balance criteria led to a continuation, followed
by reassessment and acceptance. Such records show how evidence
informs execution decisions. They do not, by themselves, establish
that model-assisted diagnosis outperforms a fixed-rule controller;
that comparison requires a separate evaluation.

\subsection{Evidence, Reporting, and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:provenance}

CFD Forge retains the artifacts needed to inspect both the simulation
and the decisions made around it. These include case configurations,
mesh information, solver logs, diagnostic measurements, model-call
records where available, proposed actions, and final assessments.
Plots, contours, and field-evolution videos connect numerical
summaries to the computed flow behavior. Field-evolution
visualizations use saved CFD fields at recorded simulation times.

The framework distinguishes live execution from replay of archived
evidence. Replay supports inspection and regression checking without
claiming that a new simulation or model call occurred. Likewise,
deterministic-backend execution is distinguished from execution using
an LLM. These distinctions are necessary when interpreting workflow
results and attributing an action to model reasoning.

Together, the execution records and scientific assessments provide an
auditable account of what was requested, what was computed, which
actions were taken, and why the result received its final decision.
The following sections examine this workflow through the nozzle,
forward-facing-step, and turbulent-cube studies.

\section{Experimental Setup and Assessment Protocol}
\label{sec:experiments}

We examine CFD Forge through two compressible-flow demonstrations
and a three-dimensional turbulent-flow diagnostic study. The
converging--diverging nozzle provides an analytical consistency
test, while the forward-facing step exercises transient
shock-capturing, conservation assessment, and controlled variations.
The turbulent cube tests whether the assessment process can
distinguish numerical completion from sufficiently developed flow.
All three studies use OpenFOAM Foundation v14.

The assessment uses preserved simulation outputs and execution
records. Case-specific criteria are applied to the underlying
numerical evidence, while model-call and action records establish
the role of agent reasoning. Reference agreement, numerical
acceptance, and physical development are treated as distinct
questions.

\subsection{Converging--Diverging Nozzle}
\label{sec:nozzle_setup}

The canonical nozzle is an inviscid, compressible perfect-gas
calculation using \texttt{shockFluid}. The gas properties are
$\gamma=1.4$ and $R=287\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$.
The inlet stagnation pressure and temperature are
$p_0=200\,\mathrm{kPa}$ and $T_0=300\,\mathrm{K}$.
The inlet, throat, and exit radii are respectively
$0.0500$, $0.0326$, and $0.0354\,\mathrm{m}$, with a total
domain length of $0.33\,\mathrm{m}$. The area ratio is
$A_e/A^*=1.179$ and the nominal pressure ratio is $p_0/p_a=6.67$.

A structured $5^\circ$ wedge mesh contains 2,112 cells,
with 132 axial and 16 radial divisions (Fig.~\ref{fig:case_geometries}a).
Slip conditions are applied at the nozzle wall, and wedge boundaries represent
axisymmetry. The inlet uses total-pressure and
total-temperature conditions with an axially constrained velocity.
The outlet uses zero-gradient
conditions for pressure, temperature, and velocity.
The nominal ambient pressure of $30\,\mathrm{kPa}$ is used
in the operating-regime assessment; it is not imposed as a
fixed outlet pressure, and the external jet is not simulated.

Quasi-one-dimensional theory supplies the initial internal
fields and the analytical comparison. It does not constrain
the subsequent CFD exit state. The numerical setup uses
Kurganov fluxes~\cite{kurganov2000central}, Minmod reconstruction,
Euler time integration, and an adaptive timestep with a target maximum
Courant number of 0.4. The canonical run advances to
$t=0.006\,\mathrm{s}$.

Assessment includes mesh validity, solver completion,
positive and finite thermodynamic fields, conservation,
stationarity, and consistency with the intended choked,
supersonic-outlet regime. Registered requirements include
a maximum steady mass-flow mismatch below $0.1\%$ over the
final 2\,ms, normalized transient continuity error
below $0.01\%$, and monitor drift and relative field changes below $0.2\%$
over the same window.
The outlet-pressure discrepancy from quasi-one-dimensional
theory must remain below $5\%$, with the remaining registered
theory-comparison quantities below $3\%$. These checks are
combined with inlet-total-condition, sonic-throat
($0.9<M_t<1.1$), supersonic-outlet (minimum face-normal Mach number above 1.05),
stagnation-enthalpy, and flux-consistency checks, twenty checks in total.
The accepted parameterized run is classified as
\texttt{PASS\_SINGLE\_MESH}; it is not itself a grid-convergence
study. The family recipe was frozen beforehand from a manually constructed,
LLM-free reference campaign of the same nozzle on four systematically refined
wedge meshes ($132\times16$ to $330\times40$ cells) and three control cases
(halved Courant number, halved wedge angle, and a 2\% startup-pressure
perturbation), whose acceptance required last-pair changes below $0.5\%$ and
control changes below $0.2\%$.

\subsection{Mach-2 Forward-Facing Step}
\label{sec:step_setup}

The step demonstration solves the two-dimensional compressible
Euler equations using \texttt{shockFluid}. The domain has
length 3 and height 1, with the step beginning at $x=0.6$
and rising to a height of 0.2. A structured mesh contains
16,128 hexahedral cells and one spanwise layer with
\texttt{empty} front and back boundaries (Fig.~\ref{fig:case_geometries}b).

The normalized inlet and initial states are
$p=1$, $T=1$, and $\mathbf{U}=(2,0,0)$; the normalized gas has unit
sound speed, so the inlet Mach number is 2. Inlet values are fixed,
the step surface is an inviscid slip boundary, and the upper and lower
channel boundaries are symmetry planes. The outlet
uses zero-gradient pressure with inlet--outlet treatment
for velocity and temperature. The scheme uses Kurganov
fluxes, van Leer reconstruction, and Euler time integration,
copied unchanged from the Foundation v14 \texttt{shockFluid/forwardStep}
tutorial and hash-checked at run time. This recipe was frozen after a manual
reproduction of the Mach-3 tutorial on the same 16,128-cell mesh, whose
large-scale shock structure is qualitatively consistent with the classical
problem~\cite{woodward1984strong}; that reproduction is not a quantitative
or mesh-independent reference solution.
The target maximum Courant number is 0.2, and the canonical
simulation advances to normalized time $t=4$, with 40 saved states.

The assessment checks planar mesh topology, the prescribed
initial state, realized supersonic inflow, field positivity,
solver health, wall impermeability, and completion of the
requested time horizon. Conservation is evaluated using
the transient balance
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}
    \int_{\Omega}\rho\,\mathrm{d}V
    +
    \int_{\partial\Omega}
    \rho\mathbf{U}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,\mathrm{d}S
    = 0,
    \label{eq:transient_mass}
\end{equation}
with discrete storage and numerical face-flux terms.
The registered normalized closure residual must remain
below $10^{-6}$, with continuity across restart segments
checked where applicable. Unequal instantaneous inlet
and outlet fluxes are therefore not interpreted as a
conservation failure when balanced by changing stored mass.

The compression diagnostic additionally requires measurable
increases in pressure, density, and temperature across the
detected front. These are directional physical checks,
not a quantitative reference-field comparison. The canonical
Mach-2 result is assessed under this numerical and physical
contract; an independent reference-field comparison is not
part of its demonstrated acceptance. The case differs from the classical
Mach-3 forward-facing-step problem~\cite{woodward1984strong} and is not
presented as a reproduction of it.

\subsection{Turbulent Cube Diagnostic Study}
\label{sec:cube_setup}

The cube study uses the Foundation-v14
\texttt{incompressibleFluid} solver with the unsteady
Reynolds-averaged Navier--Stokes equations and
$k$--$\omega$ SST closure~\cite{menter1994sst}. With cube height $H=1$,
bulk velocity $U_b=1$, and kinematic viscosity
$\nu=2.5\times10^{-5}$, the Reynolds number is
$Re_H=U_bH/\nu=40{,}000$, in a channel of height $2H$ comparable to the
configuration of Martinuzzi and Tropea~\cite{martinuzzi1993cube}.

The computational domain spans
$x/H\in[-6,16]$, $y/H\in[0,2]$, and $z/H\in[-12,12]$.
The cube occupies $x/H\in[0,1]$,
$y/H\in[0,1]$, and $z/H\in[-0.5,0.5]$.
The mesh contains 567,360 hexahedral cells (Fig.~\ref{fig:case_geometries}c).
Developed inlet velocity and turbulence profiles are mapped from a
precursor channel calculation. The floor, roof, side walls, and cube use
no-slip velocity conditions with the preserved SST wall-function treatment.
The outlet uses fixed reference pressure and zero-gradient
velocity and turbulence fields. Pressure is kinematic gauge pressure.

The calculation uses backward time integration,
linear-upwind velocity convection, upwind turbulence
convection, and a target maximum Courant number of 0.8.
The preserved campaign continues a predecessor pilot and extends to
$t^*=tU_b/H=80$; force histories are analyzed from $t^*=20$.
Development is assessed using force histories, six wake probes, block-mean
fields, and fluctuation measures rather than solver residuals alone.

The registered stationarity gate is applied to the pressure-plus-viscous
force on the cube over the final 20 time units. It requires that the
least-squares drift of each force component across the window remain below
$2\%$ of the mean streamwise force, that the mean lateral force remain below
$5\%$ of the streamwise force, and that the mean of $|F_z|$ over the second
half of the window not exceed $1.25$ times its first-half value. A
supplementary, predeclared audit examines complete lateral-force cycles
between successive upward zero crossings of $F_z$. For cycle $n$, the
amplitude is
\begin{equation}
    A_n =
    \frac{F_{z,\max,n}-F_{z,\min,n}}{2},
    \label{eq:cycle_amplitude}
\end{equation}
and the audit criterion requires the relative
change between successive comparable complete-cycle
amplitudes to remain within $10\%$. Incomplete cycles are
excluded from this comparison. Mean drag, probe statistics,
and other development indicators provide complementary
evidence. The cube is retained as a diagnostic stress test,
not as an accepted turbulent reference solution.

\subsection{Workflow Evaluation and Execution Provenance}
\label{sec:evaluation_protocol}

The archived campaign includes nozzle geometry and
operating-condition variations, together with step Mach-number,
step-height, and execution-horizon variations. These records
are examined for the sequence of diagnostic evidence,
proposed action, permitted execution, and final assessment.
They provide examples of acceptance, continuation,
correction, and safe termination.

The nozzle and step runs record their model calls explicitly.
Request interpretation, mesh-evidence review, diagnosis, and summary
calls in these runs used \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite}; the step runs
additionally invoked a Gemini multimodal field-image observer whose model
identifier was not recorded in the run provenance. The nozzle diagnosis
step receives a theory-blind evidence packet from which analytical target
values are excluded. These records are
distinguished from deterministic-backend execution and
replay of archived assessments. The cube campaign was executed outside the
agent loop and contains no model call; its analysis is
attributed to quantitative diagnostic evidence, and the
available trace does not establish that an LLM independently
discovered the growing wake mode.

The present evaluation documents demonstrated workflow
behavior and scientific decisions. Archived-case replay
is used to check consistency of recorded outcomes, not
as evidence of new autonomous CFD execution or
generalization. Controlled ablations against fixed-rule
workflows have not yet been performed; consequently,
the study does not claim a measured improvement in
success rate, computational cost, or accuracy attributable
to the LLM.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Converging--Diverging Nozzle}
\label{sec:nozzle_results}

The canonical nozzle run completed to $t=6$\,ms in 30,471 adaptive steps
(maximum Courant number 0.400) and passed all twenty registered checks,
yielding \texttt{PASS\_SINGLE\_MESH} and a deterministic \texttt{ACCEPT}.
Figure~\ref{fig:nozzle} compares radially volume-weighted axial profiles
with the isentropic quasi-one-dimensional solution for the same area
distribution. The flow accelerates from $M=0.253$ in the inlet section,
reaches $M=1.029$ in the throat, and leaves the nozzle at $M=1.513$, while
static pressure and temperature fall to $0.266\,p_0$ and $0.686\,T_0$. The
minimum slab-averaged Mach number downstream of the throat is 1.17 and the
minimum face-normal outlet Mach number is 1.46, so no internal normal shock
forms and the entire exit plane is supersonic. The exit static pressure,
53.1\,kPa, exceeds the 30\,kPa nominal ambient pressure, which is consistent
with an underexpanded nozzle.

The agent-constructed canonical run reproduces the coarsest mesh of the
LLM-free reference campaign exactly: every reported quantity, the 30,471
time steps, and the stationarity metrics agree to machine precision. The
reference refinement study (Table~\ref{tab:nozzle_grid}) passed its
registered criteria, with last-pair changes of at most $0.41\%$ (throat
Mach number) and $0.27\%$ (exit pressure). Refinement increases the exit Mach
number from 1.513 to 1.524 and moves the solution slightly further from
quasi-one-dimensional theory, to $-3.16\%$ in exit pressure on the finest
mesh, which is still within the $5\%$ limit. This trend is consistent with
resolving multidimensional corner effects that the one-dimensional model
omits. No formal grid-convergence index is reported.

A separate archived run of the same specification illustrates the
corrective loop (Fig.~\ref{fig:nozzle}c). Executed initially only to

($1.35\%$ against $0.1\%$), monitor-stationarity ($2.0\%$ against $0.2\%$),

already below $0.7\%$. Agreement with the reference therefore did not
establish that the flow had settled, and the stationarity evidence
determined the outcome. After an approved continuation to 6\,ms, all three
metrics fell below their limits (ratios 0.59, 0.41, and 0.66) and the run
was accepted.

\subsection{Mach-2 Forward-Facing Step}
\label{sec:step_results}

The canonical step run completed to $t=4$ in 5,428 steps and 102\,s of wall
time, passing all 17 hard checks and the three compression checks
(\texttt{PASS\_2D\_FORWARD\_STEP}, deterministic \texttt{ACCEPT}). The
realized inlet Mach number was 2.000006, and the maximum Courant number was
0.202 against the 0.2 controller target. Figure~\ref{fig:step} shows the
flow at the registered horizon. A detached compression front spans the
channel ahead of the step, an expansion fan forms at the step shoulder, and
an oblique shock from the shoulder region reflects from the upper symmetry
plane near $x\approx1.95$. Across the detected front, pressure, density,
and temperature rise by factors of 5.18, 2.87, and 1.81. These values exceed
the stationary normal-shock ratios for Mach 2 (4.50, 2.67, and 1.69); the
difference is qualitatively consistent with a front that is moving
upstream, but these gradient-based regional measurements are not
grid-converged and are not used as acceptance bands.

The detected front moves upstream monotonically, from $x=0.525$ at $t=0.1$
to $x=0.113$ at $t=4$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:step}c). A quasi-one-dimensional
estimate explains this behavior. Behind a normal shock at Mach 2 the flow
has $M=0.577$, whose sonic area is $0.822$ of the channel height. This
exceeds the $0.8$ opening above the step, so the subsonic post-shock flow
cannot pass the contraction and the front is displaced upstream. At Mach 3
the corresponding sonic area is $0.719$, which is why a standing detached
shock is possible in the classical Mach-3 configuration. The canonical
Mach-2 solution is therefore genuinely transient at $t=4$. The registered
contract evaluates health, conservation, and compression structure at that
horizon and applies no stationarity criterion to this family.

Mass conservation closes to round-off in the storage-aware form of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:transient_mass}). The maximum normalized closure residual is
$5.7\times10^{-11}$ (99th percentile $3.0\times10^{-11}$), against the
registered $10^{-6}$ limit (Fig.~\ref{fig:step}d). The cumulative defect is
$8\times10^{-14}$ of the initial mass, and the largest flux through the
impermeable boundaries is $1.1\times10^{-17}$. At $t=4$ the instantaneous
outlet mass flux is $16\%$ below the inlet flux, a difference balanced by
mass accumulating behind the advancing front. A test based on inlet--outlet
equality would have rejected a correctly conservative transient solution.

\subsection{Turbulent Cube Diagnostic Study}
\label{sec:cube_results}

The cube calculation completed its bounded extension to $t^*=80$ without
numerical failure. In the final two segments the maximum Courant number was
0.79, the largest reported continuity error was $2\times10^{-12}$, and the
outlet and inlet volume fluxes balanced to a relative $3\times10^{-11}$.
Instantaneous fields at $t^*=80$ show a physically plausible horseshoe
region, separated shear layers from the leading edges, and a recirculating
wake (Fig.~\ref{fig:cube}a). The streamwise force also appears settled. Over
the registered window $t^*\in[59.98, 79.98]$, the mean drag coefficient is
$C_D=2F_x=1.412$ and its drift is $0.075\%$ of the mean, well within the
$2\%$ limit, and the mean lateral force is $0.063\%$ of the drag (limit
$5\%$).

The lateral force shows that the flow has not developed
(Fig.~\ref{fig:cube}c). $F_z$ oscillates with an almost constant period of
about 10.1 time units ($St\approx0.098$), while its envelope grows: the mean
of $|F_z|$ rises from $1.83\times10^{-3}$ in the first half of the window
to $3.85\times10^{-3}$ in the second, a ratio of 2.10 against the registered
limit of 1.25. The gate therefore returns \texttt{STILL\_DEVELOPING}, and the
run is rejected. The supplementary complete-cycle audit agrees
(Fig.~\ref{fig:cube}d). Successive complete-cycle amplitudes grow by
$209\%$, $137\%$, and $113\%$, and the last comparable pair exceeds the
$10\%$ audit limit by an order of magnitude. The growth rate is declining
but the mode has not saturated. The two diagnostics are different
measurements and are reported separately. Only the half-window ratio is part
of the registered gate.

The result illustrates why the assessment cannot rely on a single signal.
Solver completion, small continuity errors, a stable mean drag, and
plausible instantaneous contours all coexist here with a lateral mode whose
amplitude more than doubles every cycle. Block-averaged fields are also
misleading: between the $t^*=60$--70 and 70--80 averages the mean drag
changes by only $0.04\%$ and wake-probe means by at most $0.002\,U_b$, while
the root-mean-square lateral force increases by $110\%$. Period averages
suppress an alternating signal whose amplitude is still growing.

\subsection{Agentic Workflow and Corrective Actions}
\label{sec:agent_results}

Table~\ref{tab:assessment} summarizes the registered outcomes, and
Table~\ref{tab:actions} lists recorded decision points in which a model
proposal met the deterministic action validator. In
the nozzle continuation, the model read the stationarity failures and
proposed \texttt{CONTINUE\_RUN}. In a Mach-3 step run that was healthy but
had reached only $t=2$ of the registered $t=4$ horizon, the model proposed
extending the end time, which the validator permitted as a bounded change
($2\to4$) before reassessment and acceptance. In a Mach-3 run with a taller
step ($h=0.3$), the deterministic compression diagnostic could no longer
measure a front; the model attributed this to upstream migration of the
shock system and proposed \texttt{FAIL\_SAFELY}. This outcome is consistent with the choking estimate
above, since the post-shock sonic area at Mach 3 (0.719) exceeds the 0.7
opening. That run was preserved and rejected rather than tuned.

One recorded run shows the validator overruling the model. A Mach-3 request
asked for a numerical-sensitivity assessment. The model first proposed
\texttt{REFINE\_MESH}, which was approved under the registered bounded policy
(linear refinement by a factor of 2, from 4,032 to 16,128 cells, as a fresh
solve). Both grids then passed their hard checks, and the model proposed
\texttt{ACCEPT}. The action validator refused this, because no cross-grid
tolerance is registered for the step family, and the requested sensitivity
assessment therefore could not be certified. The run ended as
\texttt{STOPPED\_ACTION\_REFUSED} and is recorded as a rejection, although
each individual solution was healthy.

These records demonstrate that the interface between model reasoning and
deterministic authority functions as designed, including when the model's
proposal is not admissible. In every recorded case the
terminal verdict was computed by the validators, and continuation or
extension occurred only through permitted actions. The records do not
measure whether model reasoning improves on a fixed rule. In these examples
a rule keyed to the failed check would have selected the same action, and
the multimodal observer returned no usable observation in some runs without
affecting the outcome.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The demonstration covers three CFD studies. Nozzle and forward-facing-step
flows are the validated, routable families. The cube is supporting diagnostic
evidence and is not routable. Each new physics family requires its own
scientific contract, and acceptance under a family contract is not a general
statement of CFD correctness. The accepted runs are single-mesh
verifications. The nozzle family rests on a frozen four-mesh reference
campaign, but no grid-convergence index is reported, and the step family has
neither a registered cross-grid criterion nor a quantitative reference-field
comparison. The
cube wall treatment is uncertain: at $t^*=80$ the cube-surface $y^+$ has a
median of 24.7 and a range of 2.8--96.5, with $67\%$ of the area below
$y^+=30$. A provisional comparison of its evolving mean wake with
ERCOFTAC centre-line profiles is not satisfactory, so no experimental
validation is claimed. General CAD/STEP-to-CFD execution is not
demonstrated. Finally, the evaluation documents workflow behavior rather
than an ablation. Comparisons with a fixed-rule controller and with
model-only acceptance remain future work.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

CFD Forge links engineering requests to OpenFOAM case construction,
execution, diagnosis, correction, and reporting, while keeping scientific
acceptance in deterministic, family-specific contracts. Language-model
reasoning interprets requests and evidence and proposes bounded actions;
validators decide whether those actions are admissible and whether a result
is accepted. Under this division, a converging--diverging nozzle was accepted
with sub-$2\%$ agreement with quasi-one-dimensional theory after its
stationarity evidence, not its reference agreement, determined when the run
was complete. A transient Mach-2 forward-facing-step flow was accepted with
storage-aware mass closure at round-off. A three-dimensional turbulent cube
was rejected because a growing lateral mode showed that solver completion and
a settled mean drag did not establish a developed flow. Case files, model
calls, action rulings, and field-evolution records keep each decision
auditable. These elements provide a basis for high-throughput simulation,
active learning, and design-optimization loops in which automated
evaluations must remain scientifically accountable.
